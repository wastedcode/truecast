# Beat the frontier — product marketing

Clean copy is the floor. The bar is **the best product marketer doing this kind of work today, and past
them** — the founder should never have to come back with "why should the reader care?", "this reads like
AI", "why is that internal detail in public copy?", "too many words", or "who would share this?". Every
one of those is an ask you could have answered first. You can mine every review and transcript, write 30
headlines and diff every competitor's page before you pick one; no human marketer can. Do that.

## 1. Name the frontier (seeds as of 2026-10 — search for who's leading now before you rely on them)
| Kind of work | Who sets the bar | Look at |
|---|---|---|
| Positioning | April Dunford | *Obviously Awesome*, *Sales Pitch* |
| Homepage messaging | Anthony Pierri & Robert Kaminski (Fletch PMM) | their public homepage teardowns |
| B2B marketing system | Emily Kramer (MKT1) | positioning + messaging teardowns |
| Strategic narrative | Andy Raskin | "the greatest sales deck" — name the shift in the world |
| Conversion copy from VOC | Joanna Wiebe (Copyhackers); Harry Dry (Marketing Examples) | before/after copy |
| Launch craft | Stripe, Linear, Vercel launch pages | the latest launch each has shipped |
Cover every leader relevant to *this* deliverable. Read their relevant piece now (WebFetch) and write down the
specific moves you're taking.

## 2. What their work does that competent work doesn't
- The headline names the category and the customer's alternative, not an adjective.
- Every claim carries a proof point a competitor couldn't truthfully copy.
- Copy is mined from the customer's verbatims — their words, not the team's.
- The message matches the reader's awareness stage (Schwartz).
- The real product or a demo is above the fold; claims link to proof.
- Launches are tiered by audience impact, with an asset list per tier.
- No era-stamped AI phrasing, no structured slop, no hyperbole.

## 3. Table stakes — the canon you already exceed
Dunford, *Obviously Awesome* · Moore, *Crossing the Chasm* · Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* · Ogilvy
· Raskin's strategic-narrative essay · Wiebe's VOC method. The entry ticket, not the bar.

## 4. Where you go past them
- **The composite, first:** one deliverable that carries the signature move of *every* leader in §1 at
  once — something none of them could produce alone — and then the coverage below, which none of them can
  afford. Beating any one of them is a miss.
- **Build the taste first:** study many exemplars from every leader in §1 (live), plus what expert
  critics praised and faulted; write down the moves and the flaws — that is your experience for this task.
- **Search the space:** audience segment × alternative named × core claim × proof point × headline form × narrative arc — write 30+ headlines and several full narratives, read each cold as the buyer, keep what lands. Try combinations nobody in the field has; score, keep, recombine,
  repeat until the winner beats every leader side by side. Say how many you tried.
- Fact-check **every** claim against the shipped product before it goes out.
- Write **many** variants (headline, hook, name) and pick on the brief — say why the winner won.
- Mine **every** available review, transcript and support thread for the customer's words.
- Diff the copy against **every** direct competitor's page; keep README, docs and site in sync.

## 5. The asks to pre-empt (do these before anyone asks)
- **The reader's stake first:** why should *this* reader care, in their terms, before any mechanism —
  the technical story hangs off it (`craft-the-narrative`).
- **Sounds like a person:** the AI-tell scrub run (no "honest" disclosures, no triads, no staccato, no
  hedging stacks, no hyperbole); in the founder's voice (`honor-the-voice`, `scale-with-ai-not-slop`).
- **Short:** plain words, cut to the point; visuals instead of walls of text where a picture explains.
- **Nothing internal leaks:** no stack, vendors, internal fields, tiers, codes or pipeline narration.
- **Shareable and citable:** what's in it for the person who shares it (curiosity, status, FOMO, a number
  nobody else has)? Why would a journalist cite *this* over a thousand other articles?
- **Names that work:** said aloud, misread, searched — no name that looks like a typo of a common word.
- **Consistent:** one term per concept across site, docs and product.
- **Tied to the announcement:** every piece connects back to what we're actually launching.
- **Show the payoff, not just the setup:** the page shows the product *producing the result the buyer
  hires it for* — real output, captured from a real run, with its source. If that artifact doesn't exist
  yet, specify exactly how to produce it for real and get it produced (a teammate with the tools runs the
  product); never cut the proof and never fake it. Install/safety proof is secondary to outcome proof.
- **Beat the real alternative, not a strawman:** research what the savviest buyer actually uses today —
  DIY, free/community options, incumbents, the platform's built-ins — and lead with what *only this* does
  better. If the opening promise is something they already get free, the reader leaves in five seconds.
- **The claim ledger:** list every claim, number, version, count, label ("verbatim", "only", "every",
  "never") and promise on the page with its source line. Each must be literally true *for what a visitor
  gets from the public channel the day the page ships* (published package version, default branch, tags,
  live links → 200). Unsourced numbers are cut. Understatement of risk (e.g. network access) counts as false.
- **The CTA works exactly as written:** walk the primary action in the real tool, the way the page tells
  people to do it (copy, paste, run). If the tool takes one command per submit, the page gives one command
  per copy, in order, with what the user will see at each step. Every config snippet is checked against the
  platform's official reference.
- **Hour-1 truth:** describe the first-run experience the product actually gives (read the code/docs) —
  don't undersell it as homework or oversell it as magic.
- **Every premise true for the reader:** a headline that tells the reader what they "already have" or "do"
  must be literally true of them.
- **One meaning per word:** no term used for two things, and no collision with the platform's own
  vocabulary (if the platform has "Skills" or "plans", say how yours differ or use another word).
- **Who's behind it:** an accountable maker (person/org), license, and any real adoption signal; trust
  pages without a face lose to ones with one.
- **Show the range:** examples span the audience (not every example from one role/use case).
- **Tight:** each point made once; no section restating another; no block repeated on the page.
- **The discriminating contrast on the first screen:** the one fact that separates you from the reader's
  alternative (theirs vs yours, side by side) is visible without scrolling — your side alone proves nothing.
- **Every number names what it measures:** a version, count or score is labelled with its subject (the
  product's version vs a component's); no two figures on the page can be read as contradicting each other.
- **Promises agree everywhere:** the hero's promise ("one command") matches every section that delivers it.
- **Every instruction is executable today:** each FAQ answer, workaround and "just do X" is something the
  product actually supports right now — run it or cite the code; never advise a feature that isn't wired.
- **The reader's real context:** design the path for the state they're actually in (the tool already open,
  mid-task), the exit (how to undo/remove), and the true cost (which plan/model/tier it draws on).
- **Every linked surface tells the same story:** the README, docs and listings the page links to agree with
  it (nouns, install path, claims) — update them in the same change or flag the drift.
- **Disclosure safety:** if proof reveals a vulnerability, the fix ships before or with the page, and the page
  tells existing users exactly how to get it.
- **Fair comparisons:** quote the alternative's full conclusion, not the half that flatters you — a
  skeptical reader will open the receipt. And test against the *strongest* alternative a savvy reader
  actually uses (the popular free/pro option), not the weakest one you can beat.

## 6. The critique pass — be the marketer you named
Review it in their voice: Would Dunford say the alternative and category are missing? Would Pierri flag
the hero as vague? Would Wiebe find the copy invented rather than mined? Would Raskin see no shift named?
Then run `test-the-message` and `write-the-copy`'s scrub. Fix, re-read cold as the reader, repeat until a
pass finds nothing material.

## 7. The Frontier check (end of the deliverable, ≤6 lines)
- **Benchmark:** every leading exemplars you held it against (named, linked).
- **Where this beats each of them:** concretely, one line per leader.
- **Covered:** the founder's constraints + the pre-empted asks above (N/A ones and why).
- **Critique caught:** what the last passes found and you fixed.
- **Verified:** every claim traced to a shipped capability; AI-tell scrub passed; read cold as the reader.
